How to choose a tub that fits the person and the room

Most regrets with walk-in tubs come from fit, not price. A tub can be well made and still be wrong if the seat is too low, the door opens the wrong way for the bathroom, or the fill takes longer than the person can comfortably sit. Work through these in order.

  1. The person. How do they get in and out today? Do they stand and step, pivot from a walker, or slide from a wheelchair? Ask an occupational therapist if one is already involved in their care.
  2. The space. Measure the length and width of the current tub alcove, the doorway into the bathroom, and the clear floor in front of the tub. Most standard bathrooms take a 60-inch tub; smaller and wider models exist.
  3. Seat and door. Seat height should let feet rest flat. Choose an inward or outward door based on the floor space you measured and how the person turns to sit.
  4. Fill and drain. You sit inside while the tub fills and drains. Ask for the real fill and drain time of the exact model, and whether a faster drain is offered.
  5. Hot water. Compare the tub's capacity with your water heater, especially for winter. Our winter hot-water check shows the math.
  6. Extras last. Air jets, water jets, heated seats and chromotherapy are comfort features. Pay for them only if they will be used every week.

Walk-in tub or roll-in shower?

A walk-in tub suits someone who can still step over a low threshold and wants to soak, often for joint or muscle comfort. A roll-in or curbless shower suits someone who uses a wheelchair, has trouble sitting while a tub fills and drains, or has a caregiver helping with bathing. If mobility is likely to change in the next few years, compare both before you decide. Questions Utah families ask

Does Medicare pay for a walk-in tub in Utah?

Original Medicare generally does not pay for walk-in tubs, because they are treated as home changes rather than medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans include a small home-safety benefit, so call the number on your plan card and ask. Veterans and Utahns on certain Medicaid waivers have better options, covered on our help-paying page.

Can a Utah veteran use a VA grant for a walk-in tub?

Yes, if a VA doctor prescribes it. The VA HISA grant pays up to $6,800 over a lifetime for a service-connected need or a 50 percent or higher rating, and up to $2,000 for other veterans enrolled in VA care. Utah veterans start with their care team at the George E. Wahlen VA Medical Center in Salt Lake City or one of its Utah clinics.

How long does a walk-in tub installation take?

When a walk-in tub replaces an existing tub in the same spot, many installs finish in one day. Tile repair, an old drain line or a new electrical circuit can add a day. You should know the expected schedule before you sign, and it should be written on your quote.

Will Utah hard water damage a walk-in tub?

Hard water will not ruin a tub, but it leaves mineral scale on the shell, the door seal and any jets. Salt Lake City reports total hardness from 136 to 571 milligrams per liter depending on the source, which is hard to very hard. Wipe the tub after use, run any cleaning cycle the maker recommends, and consider a softener if your home does not have one.

Is my water heater big enough for a walk-in tub?

Check two numbers before you order: the gallons the tub holds at bathing depth on its spec sheet, and the first-hour rating on your water heater label. Utah winters bring colder water into the house, so the same heater delivers less warm bath water in January than in August. If it is close, a larger heater or an inline tub heater may be needed.

Should the door swing in or out?

An inward door seals as the water pushes against it and needs less floor space in front of the tub. An outward door leaves more room inside the tub to turn and sit, which some people who use a walker or wheelchair prefer, but it needs clear floor space to open. Measure the space in front of the tub before you choose.
